Learn anything faster with an AI tutor

Research-backed study techniques, and how AI helps without thinking for you.

9 min read · 1 interactive demo

The night before an exam, you reread your notes. You highlight. You reread again. It all looks familiar — you must know it.

Then the exam starts and your mind goes blank.

That's not a personal failing. It's a well-known finding: the study methods that feel most productive often work least. Familiarity is easy to mistake for knowing. The methods that work feel harder — and that's where an AI tutor helps.

🔑Big idea

You learn by making your brain work — so let the AI ask, and you answer.

What actually works

A widely cited 2013 review by John Dunlosky and colleagues rated ten popular study techniques on the evidence. Only practice testing (quizzing yourself) and spacing out your study were rated highly useful. Highlighting and rereading were rated low.

Five techniques with solid research behind them:

Technique What it means How AI helps
Active recall Pulling an answer out of memory, not rereading Quizzes you, one question at a time
Spaced repetition Reviewing at growing gaps: tomorrow, 3 days, next week… Makes flashcards and a schedule
Explaining in your own words Teaching an idea to find your gaps Plays a curious student who spots holes
Worked examples Studying a solved problem step by step first Shows solutions, then hands over the steps
Interleaving Mixing problem types instead of blocks Builds mixed practice sets

They aren't magic. Recall and spacing are among the most consistently supported findings. Interleaving is promising but less proven, with strong results in maths and in telling similar things apart. Worked examples help most beginners; as you improve, solving problems yourself matters more.

Why "harder" works better: psychologist Robert Bjork calls these desirable difficulties. Struggling a little to recall an answer strengthens the memory more than easy re-reading. In a well-known 2006 experiment, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke found that students who practised recalling a text remembered noticeably more a week later than students who spent the same time re-reading — even though the re-readers felt more confident. (Tested five minutes later, the re-readers did slightly better, which is exactly why rereading feels like it works.) Spacing works partly because you've started to forget, so each review takes effort. Because these methods feel harder, people often wrongly believe they're working less well.

Six ways to use an AI tutor

In "Explain like I'm 5" and "Real-world examples" the AI does the talking — good for a first look. In "Socratic tutor", "Quiz me" and "Feynman check", you answer and get corrected. Those build lasting memory.

🧩A gym, not a taxi

You wouldn't ask a trainer to lift the weights for you. Use an AI tutor as a coach who sets exercises and spots your form — not a taxi to the answer.

Your AI study toolkit

One prompt per technique. Paste into a capable AI assistant and fill in the brackets.

Active recall. Close your notes and let the AI question you.

Prompt
I just studied [topic / chapter]. My notes: [paste notes].
Quiz me one question at a time, mixing facts with "why" and "how" questions. Wait for my answer.
If I'm wrong, give a hint and let me retry before explaining.
At the end, list the 3 things I should review.

Spaced repetition. AI turns notes into flashcards. The spacing is best left to a flashcard app (Anki is a popular one, free on most devices) — or an agent that can put reviews in your calendar.

Prompt
Turn these notes into 20 flashcards: [paste notes].
One idea per card; answers under 20 words. Include some "why" cards, not just definitions.
Format each as: question;answer — for import into a flashcard app.
Then suggest a 3-week review schedule with growing gaps.

Explain it in your own words. Often called the Feynman technique, popularly named after the physicist Richard Feynman, famous for explaining hard ideas simply. If you can't explain it plainly, you've found a gap.

Prompt
I'll explain [topic] in my own words, as if to a curious 12-year-old.
Play that student: question anything vague, flag anything wrong or missing, make me define jargon.
After my two tries, show a clear version and highlight what I missed.

Worked examples, then fade them out. One full solution, then more and more steps left to you.

Prompt
I'm a beginner learning [skill, e.g. "calculating percentages for discounts and tax"].
1. Show one fully worked example, explaining each step.
2. Then a similar problem with the last step left to me; then the last two; until I solve one alone.
Check each answer before moving on.

Interleaving. A shuffled set makes you practise choosing the method — just like a real exam.

Prompt
Give me 12 mixed problems on [topics, e.g. "fractions, percentages and ratios"], shuffled.
For each, ask which method I'd use and why, and correct a wrong choice before I calculate.

Learning a whole new field

New area at work, or just curious about something big? A research agent — one that searches and reads many sources for you, like the research agent example — can map the territory.

Prompt
I'm new to [field, e.g. "supply-chain management"] and need a working understanding within [time].
Research it and give me:
1. A one-page map of the main ideas and how they connect.
2. The 15 key terms, each in one plain sentence.
3. 5 well-regarded beginner resources, with links and why each helps.
4. Common beginner misconceptions.
Cite sources and note where experts disagree.

Then turn the map into a study plan — the "Study plan" mode above is a good start.

Check the facts

AI tutors are patient but not always right. As you saw in The AI brain, a model can state a wrong date, garbled formula or nonexistent source with total confidence. As a beginner, you're the least able to notice.

  • Anchor to a trusted source. Give it your textbook or notes and ask it to stick to them.
  • Ask for sources — then open them. One link you've clicked beats ten you haven't.
  • Cross-check what matters with course materials, a teacher or an expert.
  • Notice disagreements with your class. The AI may be wrong, or your course may simplify. Ask your teacher.
⚠️Don't let it think for you

Reading an AI's answer feels like learning, but you've practised reading, not producing. A 2025 study of nearly 1,000 high-school maths students in Turkey found those given a general chatbot did better in practice but worse on a later test without it. A hint-giving version largely avoided that drop. Ask for hints, try first, and follow your school's or employer's rules on AI use.

A myth to skip: you may have heard that you learn best in your personal "learning style" — visual, auditory or hands-on — and that teaching should match it. Studies that tested this properly haven't found that matching teaching to a preferred style improves learning. Use what you enjoy, but your time is better spent on recall, spacing, explaining and mixed practice, which help pretty much everyone.

🧪A 10-minute experiment

Pick something you read this week. Instead of rereading, ask an AI for five quiz questions, notes closed. Most people are surprised how much they only thought they knew.

Quick check

1The night before an exam, which study method is research most likely to support?

Practice testing (active recall) is one of the best-supported techniques. Rereading and highlighting feel productive because the material becomes familiar — but familiar isn't the same as being able to recall it.

2Which way of using an AI tutor builds the most lasting understanding?

Explaining in your own words forces you to produce the idea, which reveals and fixes gaps. Reading answers or summaries is useful for a first look, but on its own it's mostly passive.

3Your AI tutor confidently tells you a date that differs from your textbook. What should you do?

AI can state wrong facts with confidence, and as a learner you're not yet in a position to tell. Checking a trusted source — or asking your teacher — settles it.
